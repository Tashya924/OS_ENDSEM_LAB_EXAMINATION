EXPLANATION -- Question 3: Deadlock Prevention via Resource Ordering
======================================================================

Why the bad version creates circular wait:
  Process A acquires Lock1 first and then tries to acquire Lock2.
  Process B acquires Lock2 first and then tries to acquire Lock1.
  If both processes get past their first acquisition before either
  reaches its second acquisition (guaranteed here by the deliberate
  delay between acquisitions), each process ends up holding the
  resource the other one needs next: A holds Lock1 and waits on Lock2,
  while B holds Lock2 and waits on Lock1. This is a circular wait of
  length 2 (A -> B -> A in the wait-for graph), and combined with
  mutual exclusion, hold-and-wait, and no preemption, it is a deadlock.

How resource ordering eliminates circular wait:
  A total order is imposed on the resources: Lock1 < Lock2. Every
  process is required to request resources only in increasing order,
  and must acquire a lower-ordered resource before ever requesting a
  higher-ordered one. Under this rule no process can hold a
  higher-ordered resource while requesting a lower-ordered one, so a
  cycle of processes each waiting on the next can never form -- the
  circular-wait condition is structurally impossible.

Why consistent ordering prevents deadlock:
  Coffman's four necessary conditions for deadlock are mutual
  exclusion, hold-and-wait, no preemption, and circular wait. Deadlock
  requires all four to hold simultaneously. Resource ordering removes
  circular wait unconditionally (by construction, not by luck of
  timing), so even though the other three conditions may still hold,
  deadlock cannot occur. This is exactly what the fixed-version run
  below demonstrates: identical workload, identical delay between
  acquisitions, but both processes now request Lock1 before Lock2 and
  the run completes normally.
